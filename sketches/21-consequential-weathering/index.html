consequential weathering

When we ask ‘Who has the right to control and change the weather?’, we should also ask ‘Who is already interfering in the weather at large scales?’ Because can we really achieve and talk about a weather commons, if all systems it is entangled with are extracted and manipulated across scales?

This sketch will look at extractive landscapes, especially open-pit mines and post-mining lakes, and how they already interfere with the atmosphere, from the ground up through its vertical layers. A weather commons must address this too.

Simulate

A side view of the air above a 20 km line across Partwitzer See, from the ground up to 4 km, through one day. On the left, 1991, when this was an open pit; on the right, 2025, when it is a lake. Both run at the same time.

The ground moves the air in two ways. By friction: smooth water brakes the wind less than the bare floor of a pit, and much less than forest, so near the water the wind is faster and churns the air less. Where the wind speeds up or slows down, at shores and forest edges, the air is pushed down or up. And by heat: warm ground or water heats the air above it, which rises and churns; cool water calms the air above it. Rising air cools until its water vapour condenses into cloud, and cloud water can turn into rain. Over water much warmer than the air, the water steams.

Each year has its own day, the day a satellite photographed the ground: 7 August 1991 and 12 August 2025, both hot and dry; 26 October 1991 and 8 November 2025, both cold, calm and clear. The 2025 weather was measured at Hoyerswerda, 8 km from the lake. For 1991 there is no station that close, so the weather near the lake is estimated from Cottbus, 31 km away. To see only what the ground changes, put both on the same weather.

Modelled, approximate. Not a forecast. Ground and 2025 station readings are observed; the 1991 weather near the lake is estimated (modelled) from observed readings at Cottbus; the starting air profile is synthetic. Heights are stretched about 3 times.

What the first runs show. In summer the lake, cooler than the land, calms the air above it: less turbulence than over the land around it, and air sinking above the lake in the afternoon while it rises over the warm land. No cloud forms in either year, as observed. In autumn the lake, about 9 °C, is much warmer than the night air: it churns the air above it and steams. On 8 November 2025 steam fog covers all the open water in the Partwitzer See area through the night, about two thirds of the area. In 1991 the pit already held groundwater pools, about a third of the area, at about 7 °C. On the same weather they steam too; on the estimated 1991 night, only around dawn, all night if the night was colder than typical, and not at all if it was milder. The bigger difference is how much open water there is. The model's own mist, with its 62 m layers, stays below the level it counts.

Ground strip: lines are water, dots are bare ground, grey is vegetation. Grey in the air is cloud or mist; wavy lines just above the water are steam fog. Sources and limits

  • Ground along the line (water, bare ground, vegetation, surface temperature): observed. USGS/NASA Landsat Collection 2 Level-2, via Microsoft Planetary Computer. Public domain. Summer: 7 August 1991 (Landsat 5) and 12 August 2025 (Landsat 9). Autumn: 26 October 1991 (Landsat 5) and 8 November 2025 (Landsat 8).
  • Air temperature, dew point, pressure and wind through each day: observed. Deutscher Wetterdienst, hourly readings, CC BY 4.0. Station 00880 Cottbus, 31 km from the lake, for 7 August and 26 October 1991 and for 12 August and 8 November 2025. Station 07393 Hoyerswerda, 8 km from the lake (records from 2010), for the 2025 days.
  • Weather near the lake in 1991: estimated, modelled. Cottbus's observed readings plus the average difference between Hoyerswerda and Cottbus in 2010–2024 at the same hour, time of year, cloud cover and wind. Checked on the 2025 days, which were not used to learn: about 1 °C off in summer; on 8 November 2025 the estimate's night low was +2.0 °C, the colder version's −0.8 °C, and Hoyerswerda measured −1.6 °C, a night colder than 1 in 10 similar ones.
  • How rough each surface is: typical roughness lengths from the literature (for example Stull 1988, Wieringa 1993), approximate: water 0.2 mm, bare pit floor 3 cm, vegetation (mostly pine forest and fields) 50 cm.
  • Steam fog: estimated with the forecasters' mixing check, from the water's surface temperature (Landsat) and the air's temperature and dew point (station or estimate).
  • Starting profile of the air above the ground (temperature and moisture with height): synthetic, typical of a clear summer morning or a clear, calm autumn night, built from each day's station readings. To be replaced with real weather balloon soundings.
  • Air movement, cloud, mist and rain: modelled. A simplified 2D model with a warm-rain scheme after Kessler (1969). Approximate, not a forecast.
  • Dust effect on rain: modelled, approximate. More dust means more, smaller droplets, so rain starts later.
  • The question above: interpretive. A working draft.

What this sketch cannot show: a real forecast or hindcast, ice and hail, or the full range of droplet sizes. The air is a 2D slice, so air can't move along the lake's other direction. On each year's own day, the weather differs as well as the ground, so a difference between the panels can't be put down to the ground alone; "Both on the 2025 day" keeps the weather the same, with the 1991 ground lined up by its vegetation temperature. One station (or estimate) drives the whole 20 km line. The 1991 estimate gives a typical day near the lake; a single night can differ by several degrees, which is why the colder and milder versions are there. The wind near the ground over each surface comes from the station's wind and the log wind profile, adjusting over about 800 m after a shore or forest edge; the pit's depth and slopes are not in the model. Real steam fog is often only metres deep, thinner than the model's lowest layer. The lake temperature is held at its value at the satellite's pass (about 11:00 local time) all day. The left and right edges of the model act as walls, so less air rises near them. Each run starts with small random nudges, the same in both panels; a restart gives new ones, and results differ a little from run to run.

The steam fog over the lake on 8 November 2025 is estimated only: both stations are on land and reported clear sky, so there is no observation of it to check against yet.
